NGUYEN V. TRENT INSTITUTE OF TECHNOLOGY
Nguyen, Plaintiff,
v.
Trent Institute of Technology, Defendant.

Citation: 98 F.4th 214 (4th Cir. 2024)
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit
Decision Date: 2024-05-06
Opinion Type: Majority
Author: Judge Foster Langtry Jr.
Status: active
Topic: Essay-Based Proxy Consideration

========================================================================================

Procedural History
------------------

Plaintiff Michael Nguyen sued Trent Institute of Technology -- the same institution
whose earlier admissions programs were addressed in Delgado, Prentiss, and Park --
alleging that the University's post-Harmon essay-scoring rubric violated Harmon by
awarding additional points for essays that referenced the applicant's racial identity.

Facts
-----

After Harmon, Trent Institute of Technology adopted an essay-scoring rubric that awarded
up to five additional points, out of a fifty-point essay scale, to essays that
'meaningfully engage with the applicant's racial or ethnic identity.' The rubric applied
the bonus based on whether the essay referenced race at all, without regard to whether
the essay's substantive content otherwise reflected individual character, perspective,
or achievement.

Issue(s) Presented
------------------

Whether an essay-scoring rubric that awards points based on whether an essay references
race, rather than based on the substance of what the essay reveals about the applicant,
is consistent with Harmon's distinction between considering how race affected an
applicant's life and considering race itself.

Standard of Review
------------------

This case is governed by strict scrutiny, under which a race-conscious government policy
is sustained only if it is narrowly tailored to further a compelling governmental
interest.

Legal Analysis
--------------

The panel reads Harmon's essay clarification narrowly: an institution may credit an
essay for what it reveals about the applicant's individual experience, character, or
perspective, even where that experience relates to race, but may not award credit simply
because an essay references race, since doing so treats race itself, rather than the
individualized content of the essay, as the operative criterion.

The panel finds Trent Institute's rubric falls on the impermissible side of that line
because it awards a fixed point bonus keyed explicitly to whether the essay 'engages
with racial or ethnic identity,' without any accompanying instruction to evaluate the
substantive individual content of the essay. In the panel's view, this is functionally
similar to the essay-reading practice enjoined in Odom v. Milford University, applying a
uniform adjustment based on the presence of race-related content rather than on
individualized substance.

The panel rejects the University's argument that its rubric merely operationalizes the
flexibility Harmon itself preserved, reasoning that Harmon's clarification was limited
to allowing consideration of how race affected an applicant's life as part of an
individualized assessment of the whole essay, not to permitting a separate, race-
triggered point bonus layered on top of that assessment.

The panel enjoins the rubric and instructs Trent Institute that essay evaluation must
focus on the individualized substance of each essay without regard to whether it
references race.

Discussion of Prior Cases
-------------------------

* Harmon v. Board of Trustees of Ashworth University, 601 U.S. 118 (2023) -- this opinion follows that authority. Applies harmon's narrow essay clarification.
* Odom v. Milford University, 404 F. Supp. 3d 812 (D. Fentress 2019) -- this opinion follows that authority. Finds the rubric analogous to the uniform adjustment enjoined in odom.
* Office for Civil Rights Frequently Asked Questions on Admissions Practices After Harmon, OCR FAQ 2024-01 -- this opinion follows that authority. Follows the office for civil rights' recommendation to focus on substance rather than the presence of race-related content.

Holding
-------

The Fourth Circuit holds that Trent Institute's rubric impermissibly considers race
itself, because it awards points based on the mere presence of racial-identity content
rather than on the substance of what any essay -- race-referencing or not -- reveals
about the applicant.

Conclusion
----------

The judgment of the district court is REVERSED, and the essay-scoring rubric is
ENJOINED.

CALLOWAY V. ASHWORTH STATE UNIVERSITY
Calloway, Petitioner,
v.
Ashworth State University, Respondent.

Citation: 476 U.S. 512 (1986)
Court: Supreme Court of the United States
Decision Date: 1986-06-24
Opinion Type: Dissent
Author: Justice Hollis Vance
Status: active
Topic: Strict Scrutiny - Colorblind Constitution

========================================================================================

Procedural History
------------------

Petitioner Daniel Calloway, a rejected applicant to Ashworth State University, sued in
the United States District Court for the District of Fentress, alleging that the
University's holistic admissions process nonetheless functioned as an informal quota
because the percentage of minority students in each entering class had remained within a
narrow band for six consecutive years. The district court and the Fourth Circuit both
rejected the claim, applying Whitfield and Brennan City. This Court granted certiorari
to clarify the narrow-tailoring inquiry left open in Whitfield.

Facts
-----

Ashworth State University's admissions office reviews each application individually and
considers race as one of many factors. Over six consecutive admissions cycles, the
percentage of the entering class identifying as a member of a designated minority group
ranged between eleven and thirteen percent. Calloway argued this statistical stability
was itself evidence of an unwritten quota functioning beneath a facially individualized
process.

Issue(s) Presented
------------------

Whether stare decisis should preserve Whitfield's diversity rationale notwithstanding
the dissent's view that it was wrongly decided.

Standard of Review
------------------

This case is governed by strict scrutiny, under which a race-conscious government policy
is sustained only if it is narrowly tailored to further a compelling governmental
interest.

Dissenting Opinion
------------------

Justice Vance reiterates the objections he raised twelve years earlier in Whitfield:
that no interest, however sincerely held, justifies classifying an individual applicant
by race, and that the majority's diversity rationale rests on an assumption of group-
based viewpoint that the Equal Protection Clause forbids government from making.

Justice Vance further argues that today's three-part narrow-tailoring test, however
carefully constructed, cannot cure what he views as a foundational defect: a test built
to police the degree of race-consciousness still permits race-consciousness, and no
degree of procedural safeguard changes that basic constitutional calculus in his view.

On stare decisis, Justice Vance writes that the doctrine is at its weakest when a
decision, like Whitfield, departs from the text and history of the constitutional
provision it purports to interpret. In his view, the passage of twelve years and the
reliance institutions have placed on Whitfield do not, by themselves, transform an
erroneous reading of the Equal Protection Clause into a correct one.

Justice Vance closes by expressing his view that a future Court will eventually
recognize what he believes Whitfield got wrong: that the Constitution's promise of equal
protection is honored only by a rule that forbids government from classifying
individuals by race for any purpose.

Discussion of Prior Cases
-------------------------

* Whitfield v. Board of Regents of Milford University, 417 U.S. 226 (1974) -- this opinion affirms that authority. Reiterates the colorblind-constitution position first stated in the whitfield dissent.

Holding
-------

Justice Vance, dissenting for the second time in this line of cases, argues that
Whitfield was wrongly decided and that stare decisis should not preserve a rule
permitting government to sort individuals by race.

Conclusion
----------

Justice Vance respectfully dissents.

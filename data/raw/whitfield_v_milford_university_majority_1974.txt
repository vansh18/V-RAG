WHITFIELD V. BOARD OF REGENTS OF MILFORD UNIVERSITY
Whitfield, Petitioner,
v.
Board of Regents of Milford University, Respondent.

Citation: 417 U.S. 226 (1974)
Court: Supreme Court of the United States
Decision Date: 1974-06-10
Opinion Type: Majority
Author: Justice Alden T. Marsh
Status: limited
Overruled/Superseded By: Harmon v. Board of Trustees of Ashworth University, 601 U.S. 118 (2023)
Topic: Compelling Interest - Diversity Rationale

========================================================================================

Procedural History
------------------

Petitioner Eleanor Whitfield, a rejected applicant to Milford University's undergraduate
program, filed suit in the United States District Court for the District of Fentress
alleging that the University's consideration of race in admissions violated the Equal
Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. The district court applied the rational-
basis standard set out in Marsh v. Board of Regents of Calloway State College, 412 F.2d
118 (4th Cir. 1971) (fictional), and entered judgment for the University. The Fourth
Circuit affirmed on the same rationale. This Court granted certiorari to resolve whether
race-conscious admissions programs in public higher education are subject to strict
scrutiny.

Facts
-----

Milford University's admissions office reviews each application individually and
considers an applicant's race as one factor among several in assembling an entering
class the University describes as diverse along multiple dimensions, including but not
limited to race. No fixed number of seats is set aside for any racial group, and race is
not assigned a predetermined numerical weight. Whitfield, who is white, was denied
admission in a year in which the University admitted a number of minority applicants
whose standardized test scores and grade point averages were, on the summary record
before the Court, lower than Whitfield's.

Issue(s) Presented
------------------

Whether a public university's individualized, race-conscious admissions program is
subject to strict scrutiny, and if so, whether the University's asserted interest in a
diverse student body can qualify as compelling.

Standard of Review
------------------

This case is governed by strict scrutiny, under which a race-conscious government policy
is sustained only if it is narrowly tailored to further a compelling governmental
interest.

Opinion of the Court
--------------------

The Court first rejects the rational-basis approach applied below. Whatever force the
Fourth Circuit's distinction between remedial and exclusionary classifications may carry
as a matter of policy, it finds no footing in the text of the Equal Protection Clause,
which speaks in terms of persons, not groups, and protects 'any person' from denial of
equal protection. The Fourteenth Amendment's guarantee cannot reasonably be read to
fluctuate in stringency depending on the race of the individual burdened by a given
classification. Marsh v. Board of Regents of Calloway State College is therefore
disapproved to the extent it applied rational-basis review to a race-conscious
admissions program.

Having settled on strict scrutiny as the governing standard, the Court turns to whether
Milford University's asserted interest in the educational benefits of a diverse student
body is capable, as a matter of law, of being compelling. The Court concludes that it
is. A university's educational mission is a special concern of the First Amendment, and
this Court has long recognized that the 'robust exchange of ideas' central to that
mission benefits from a student body assembled with an eye toward a variety of
viewpoints, experiences, and backgrounds, of which race is one component among many.

The Court is careful to cabin this holding. It does not hold that diversity for its own
sake, untethered to any showing of educational benefit, will suffice, nor does it hold
that race may be considered in isolation from the many other factors that bear on an
applicant's likely contribution to campus discourse. Nor does the Court foreclose the
possibility -- pressed by the concurrence -- that a university's interest in remedying
identified past discrimination might independently qualify as compelling in an
appropriate case; the Court simply does not reach that question on this record.

Turning to narrow tailoring, the Court finds the record before it insufficiently
developed to say with confidence whether Milford's particular admissions process
satisfies that requirement, and it remands for the lower courts to examine, among other
things, whether race is truly one factor among many rather than a determinative or
mechanically weighted criterion, and whether workable race-neutral alternatives were
seriously considered and rejected before race was used at all.

The Court emphasizes that today's holding does not endorse race consciousness in
admissions as a permanent feature of American higher education. Race-conscious remedies
of the kind at issue here are, by their nature, a temporary bridge to a system in which
such measures are no longer necessary, and courts applying strict scrutiny should remain
alert to whether a given program continues to serve a genuine and substantiated
educational purpose.

Discussion of Prior Cases
-------------------------

* Marsh v. Board of Regents of Calloway State College, 412 F.2d 118 (4th Cir. 1971) -- this opinion overrules that authority. Disapproves the fourth circuit's application of rational-basis review to race-conscious admissions programs.

Holding
-------

The Court holds that race-conscious admissions programs are subject to strict scrutiny
regardless of the race of the applicants burdened, but that a university's genuine
interest in the educational benefits that flow from a diverse student body can
constitute a compelling governmental interest, so long as the means used to pursue that
interest are narrowly tailored.

Conclusion
----------

Because the Fourth Circuit erred in applying rational-basis review, its judgment is
VACATED and the case is REMANDED for further proceedings, including a searching narrow-
tailoring inquiry, consistent with this opinion.

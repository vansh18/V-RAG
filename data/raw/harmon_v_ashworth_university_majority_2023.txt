HARMON V. BOARD OF TRUSTEES OF ASHWORTH UNIVERSITY
Harmon, Petitioner,
v.
Board of Trustees of Ashworth University, Respondent.

Citation: 601 U.S. 118 (2023)
Court: Supreme Court of the United States
Decision Date: 2023-06-29
Opinion Type: Majority
Author: Chief Justice Rosalind Delacombe
Status: active
Topic: Overruling of Diversity Rationale

========================================================================================

Procedural History
------------------

Petitioner Diane Harmon, denied admission to Ashworth University, sued in federal
district court, urging the court to disregard Reyes and Calloway as wrongly decided. The
district court, bound by this Court's precedent, upheld Ashworth's admissions process,
and the Tenth Circuit affirmed on the strength of its own prior decision in Castellano
v. Ashworth University. This Court granted certiorari, together with Harmon v. Board of
Trustees of Milford University, to reconsider Whitfield, Calloway, Reyes, and Voss.

Facts
-----

Ashworth University's admissions process in 2023 is substantially the same qualitative,
individualized process this Court approved in Reyes in 2003 and the Tenth Circuit
approved, on a developed evidentiary record concerning race-neutral alternatives, in
Castellano in 2017. The parties do not dispute that Ashworth's process, if Reyes remains
good law, satisfies the narrow-tailoring framework Reyes, Ferreira, and Castellano
together describe. Harmon instead argues that Reyes and the diversity rationale on which
it rests should be overruled.

Issue(s) Presented
------------------

Whether Whitfield, Calloway, Reyes, and Voss should be overruled, and whether the Equal
Protection Clause permits any consideration of an individual applicant's race in
university admissions.

Standard of Review
------------------

This case is governed by strict scrutiny, under which a race-conscious government policy
is sustained only if it is narrowly tailored to further a compelling governmental
interest.

Opinion of the Court
--------------------

The Court begins by revisiting the stare decisis analysis that has preserved Whitfield's
diversity rationale for nearly fifty years. The Court concludes that special
justification exists to overrule that line of cases: the diversity rationale, as this
Court's own decisions in Ferreira and the lower courts' struggles in Whitcombe and Odom
illustrate, has proven unworkable in practice, requiring an ever more elaborate
apparatus of documentation requirements, workability showings, and file-by-file
narrative review that lower courts have applied inconsistently for two decades.

The Court further concludes that Whitfield and its progeny were, in the majority's view,
wrongly decided at the outset. The Equal Protection Clause, the Court reasons, protects
persons, not groups, and its guarantee is not satisfied by a process that considers an
individual applicant's race for even benign or well-intentioned reasons. The Court finds
persuasive the reasoning first offered in Justice Vance's dissents in Whitfield and
Calloway: that no interest, however sincerely pursued, can justify sorting individual
applicants by race, because doing so is itself the harm the Fourteenth Amendment was
adopted to prevent.

The Court rejects the argument, accepted in Reyes, that the educational benefits of
diversity can qualify as a compelling interest. The Court reasons that such benefits,
whatever their value, are not sufficiently measurable or coherent to permit the kind of
searching, end-focused review strict scrutiny demands, and notes that the 'critical
mass' concept approved in Reyes was, as Justice Halvorsen warned in dissent at the time,
never capable of principled judicial administration.

The Court also rejects Justice Okafor's alternative remedial rationale from her
Whitfield concurrence, explaining that even a remedial interest, tailored to an
institution's own documented history of discrimination, would require the same kind of
racial classification of individual applicants that the majority holds the Equal
Protection Clause forbids.

The Court holds that institutions remain free to pursue student-body diversity through
means that do not classify individual applicants by race -- including the socioeconomic-
status-based and geographic alternatives piloted in Castellano, expanded outreach and
recruitment, and the elimination of preferences, such as legacy admissions, that may
correlate with race without directly classifying by it -- but may not consider an
individual applicant's race itself, in any way, as a factor in the admissions decision.

The Court clarifies that nothing in today's opinion prohibits universities from
considering an applicant's discussion of how race has affected the applicant's own life,
so long as that discussion is treated as any other aspect of the applicant's background
or character would be, and not as a proxy for the applicant's race as such -- a
distinction the Court acknowledges will require careful application in future cases,
including the essay-based challenges considered by the lower courts in Whitcombe and
Odom.

The Court closes by acknowledging the substantial reliance institutions have placed on
Whitfield, Calloway, Reyes, and Voss over nearly fifty years, but concludes, consistent
with this Court's stare decisis doctrine, that reliance interests cannot preserve a rule
of constitutional law the Court now believes to be incorrect.

Discussion of Prior Cases
-------------------------

* Whitfield v. Board of Regents of Milford University, 417 U.S. 226 (1974) -- this opinion overrules that authority. Overrules whitfield's holding that the diversity rationale can qualify as a compelling interest.
* Whitfield v. Board of Regents of Milford University, 417 U.S. 226 (1974) -- this opinion overrules that authority. Rejects the alternative remedial rationale of the whitfield concurrence.
* Whitfield v. Board of Regents of Milford University, 417 U.S. 226 (1974) -- this opinion follows that authority. Adopts the colorblind-constitution reasoning first stated in the whitfield dissent.
* Calloway v. Ashworth State University, 476 U.S. 512 (1986) -- this opinion overrules that authority. Overrules calloway's three-part narrow-tailoring test as resting on an impermissible premise.
* Calloway v. Ashworth State University, 476 U.S. 512 (1986) -- this opinion follows that authority. Adopts the reasoning of justice vance's calloway dissent.
* Reyes v. Trustees of Ashworth University, 539 U.S. 306 (2003) -- this opinion overrules that authority. Overrules reyes's approval of the qualitative critical-mass framework.
* Reyes v. Trustees of Ashworth University, 539 U.S. 306 (2003) -- this opinion follows that authority. Credits justice halvorsen's warning that critical mass could not be principled administered.
* Voss v. Milford University, 539 U.S. 344 (2003) -- this opinion overrules that authority. Overrules voss to the extent it left any race-conscious admissions practice intact.
* Ferreira v. Ashworth University, 779 F.3d 240 (2d Cir. 2015) -- this opinion criticizes that authority. Cites the elaborate workability apparatus required by ferreira as evidence the diversity rationale proved unworkable.
* Castellano v. Ashworth University, 872 F.3d 1188 (10th Cir. 2017) -- this opinion criticizes that authority. Notes that the diversity rationale approved in castellano required an increasingly elaborate evidentiary showing.
* Whitcombe v. Harwick College, 481 F.3d 950 (8th Cir. 2007) -- this opinion criticizes that authority. Cites whitcombe's inconsistent lower-court application of the narrow-tailoring framework.
* Odom v. Milford University, 404 F. Supp. 3d 812 (D. Fentress 2019) -- this opinion criticizes that authority. Cites odom as an example of the essay-based line-drawing difficulties the diversity rationale produced.

Holding
-------

The Court overrules Whitfield, Calloway, Reyes, and Voss, and holds that university
admissions programs may not consider an applicant's race, even as one factor among many
within an individualized process, because doing so cannot be reconciled with the Equal
Protection Clause's guarantee that no person be treated differently on account of race.

Conclusion
----------

The judgment of the Tenth Circuit is REVERSED.
